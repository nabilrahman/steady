    Quick reference: her, you and warning signs at every stage

    Birth day: your jobs, minute by minute

    In 60 seconds

    1. Call when her provider's rule is met (often 5-1-1). Go in now for bleeding, fluid that's green or brown, less movement, constant pain, or before 37 weeks.
    2. Your job: protect her calm, handle logistics, speak for her plan, stay with her.
    3. For any non-urgent decision ask: benefits, risks, alternatives, and what if we wait?
    4. If she can't do skin-to-skin, ask to do it yourself.

    Your role on the day is simple to say and hard to do: protect her calm, carry the logistics, speak up for what she wants, and stay with her. You don't need to know medicine. You need to know the plan, the route, and when to call.

    When to go in

    A common rule is 5-1-1: contractions about every 5 minutes, each lasting about 1 minute, for 1 hour. Some providers use 4-1-1 or 3-1-1, and want you in sooner for a second baby because labor is often faster. Ask her provider which rule they want, and write it in your plan.

    Go in now, whatever the contractions are doing, if her waters break (especially green, brown or smelly fluid), she's bleeding, the baby is moving less, she has constant severe pain, she's under 37 weeks, or her gut says go.

    The hospital stay: who knocks, and can you say no?

    In 60 seconds

    1. Expect checks every few hours, a pediatrician visit, a lactation consultant, and paperwork.
    2. You can always decline sales visits and the photographer.
    3. Most medical tests can be refused only by signing a form; ask what each is for first.
    4. Write down the after-hours number and the first pediatrician appointment before you leave.

    The first one to three days are a parade of visitors, tests and forms, usually at 2 a.m. Knowing who's coming and why takes most of the stress out. In general, parents can decline or delay non-medical services, and can ask questions about, delay, and in most places refuse medical procedures, though some are required by law and refusing may mean signing a form.

    Your two magic sentences: "Can you tell me what this is for and what happens if we wait?" and "We'd like a few minutes to talk about it." Unless it's an emergency, both are always fine.

    Know your rights

    In 60 seconds

    1. She decides about her body; you decide together for the baby.
    2. Not married? You'll be offered a paternity form at the hospital. Read it before signing.
    3. Check leave now: FMLA, your state's paid leave, and your employer's policy.
    4. Add the baby to insurance quickly (often within 30 days).

    Not legal advice, just the map. Rules differ by state and country and change often, so check the official source for yours (see Sources & terms), and talk to a family-law attorney for anything complex.

    Your body, your mind, your plate

    In 60 seconds

    1. Weight gain and sympathy symptoms in dads are real and common.
    2. About 1 in 10 dads get depressed; in men it often looks like anger, withdrawal or numbing out.
    3. Two weeks of feeling bad is the signal to reach out.
    4. Batch-cook before the birth; protein, fiber and water at every meal.

    Yes, dads get a belly too

    Many expectant fathers gain weight during the pregnancy, and the weight tends to stay: one study found fathers weigh on average about 14 pounds (6 kg) more than childless men. Some of it is shared snacks and takeout; some is lost sleep, which reliably drives weight gain; and researchers have wondered if part of it is biological, since expectant marmoset and tamarin fathers gain weight too.

    Some men develop couvade syndrome ("sympathetic pregnancy"): nausea, heartburn, back ache, appetite changes and poor sleep. A 2024 study linked bigger shifts in men's prolactin and testosterone to more of these symptoms. It's real, it's not weakness, and it passes.

    Your hormones change

    New fathers who do hands-on care show falling testosterone and rising prolactin and oxytocin. Studies also find the brains of involved fathers change after the birth, linked to empathy and attention to the baby. The more you hold, feed and soothe, the stronger it gets.

    Your mental health

    About one in ten fathers gets depressed in pregnancy or the first year, and about one in ten has serious anxiety. In men it often looks different: irritability, anger, working late, numbing out with screens or drink, withdrawing, physical aches, rather than sadness.

    Get help if any of that lasts more than two weeks, you feel hopeless, you're drinking more, you feel like running, or you have any thought of harming yourself or the baby. That's not failing; it's your cue to reach out. I can also set up a session with a licensed therapist.

    A 3-minute reset for heightened moments

    • Box breathing: in 4, hold 4, out 4, hold 4. Four rounds.
    • Name it: "I'm scared because I can't fix this." Naming a feeling calms it.
    • Shrink the job: "What's the next one thing?" Water, a cold cloth, a question.
    • Step out, come back: 60 seconds in the hall is fine. Tell her you'll be right back.

    Meal plan for dads (and the whole house)

    The goal isn't dieting. It's keeping your energy steady through broken sleep and keeping her fed, especially if she's breastfeeding and hungry around the clock. Batch-cook in the third trimester; aim for protein, fiber and water at every meal; keep one-hand snacks where you feed the baby; and go easy on alcohol, which wrecks the little sleep you get.

    Feeding her after the birth

    • A full water bottle every time she sits to feed
    • Warm, soft foods: soups, stews, porridge
    • Iron-rich foods if she lost a lot of blood
    • Fibre and fluids: constipation after birth is common
    • Ask her care team about any foods to avoid, and her own cultural traditions

    Move a little

    Twenty minutes walking with the stroller counts. So does ten push-ups while the bottle warms. Small and daily beats heroic and rare.

    Eyes open: what uninformed parents get caught by

    In 60 seconds

    1. Ask before the birth whether the hospital drug tests, with consent, and who sees results.
    2. Ask for the financial assistance policy; many eligible families are never told.
    3. You never have to sign away surprise-billing protections; ask for itemized bills.
    4. You can say no to marketing bags and sales visits; if you're not heard, ask for the patient advocate.

    Most nurses and doctors are kind, overworked people doing their best. But hospitals are also businesses and institutions with routines, and reporters and researchers have documented practices that hurt families who don't know their rights.     Lifesavers, and the stuff that gathers dust
    
  
    After two babies, here's my honest list. The essentials are fewer than the internet says, and a few popular products aren't safe for sleep at all.
    
  
    
    
    Absolute lifesavers
      
    
      Barely used
        
  
        
  
        Never for sleep: inclined sleepers (more than 10°), crib bumpers, weighted swaddles, sleep sacks or blankets, positioners, nests and pods, pillows and loose bedding. Car seats, swings, bouncers and nursing pillows aren't for sleep either. The American Academy of Pediatrics says: on the back, on a flat firm surface, nothing else in the crib, in your room for at least the first six months, and never asleep with baby on a sofa or armchair.
        
  
        Baby registry
        
  
        A registry lets family buy what you actually need for the shower. The completion discount on whatever's left near the due date is usually the best perk.
